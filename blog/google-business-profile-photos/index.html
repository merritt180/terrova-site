Google Business Profile Photos: How Many You Need and What to Post

Before anyone calls your office, they look. They tap your listing on Google, swipe through your photos for about ten seconds, and decide whether you look like a business worth trusting with a root canal, a custom home, or a weekend in a car that costs more than most people's mortgage.

Photos are the most visible part of your Google Business Profile and the part most owners never touch after setup. Google's own guidance is direct about it. Listings with photos receive significantly more requests for directions and more clicks through to the website than listings without them. That is not a ranking trick. That is customers deciding.

Here is what actually belongs on your profile, how much of it you need, and how to keep it from going stale.

How many photos is enough

There is no magic number, but there is a floor. A profile with fewer than ten photos looks abandoned. A profile with thirty or more looks like a real operation. In most of the audits we run, established businesses have somewhere between three and eight photos, and half of those were uploaded by customers.

Aim for at least twenty five photos, then add a few every month. The count matters less than the impression. When someone swipes and keeps finding something new, they stop evaluating and start booking.

The photos that actually do work

Google gives you several photo categories, and each one answers a question the customer is already asking.

Exterior shots. These answer "will I be able to find this place." Photograph the front of your building from the street, from the direction most people approach, in daylight. Include the sign. A med spa tucked inside an office plaza on Ventura Boulevard should show the plaza entrance, not just the suite door, because that is what the customer will be looking at from their car.

Interior shots. These answer "what am I walking into." For a dental practice, that means the waiting room, the operatory, and the front desk. Clean, bright, no clutter, no people mid task. For a law firm, a conference room says more than a stock photo of a gavel ever will.

Team photos. These answer "who am I dealing with." People hire people. One good photo of the dentist, the builder, or the attorney standing in their own space builds more trust than any headline on your website. Faces outperform buildings almost every time.

Work product. This is the category most businesses underuse and the one that closes. A home builder should have twenty finished project photos, exteriors and interiors, in daylight. An exotic car rental should have every vehicle in the fleet photographed well, because clients search for the car, not the company. A med spa can show the treatment rooms and the equipment without ever crossing a line into medical claims.

Your logo and cover photo. These are the two Google leans on most in search results. The logo should be square and clean. The cover photo should be your single best image, because Google often uses it as the first thing anyone sees.

What to leave off

Stock photography is the fastest way to look like every other business in your category. Customers recognize it instantly, even when they cannot say why something feels off. Blurry phone photos taken at night are worse than no photo. Screenshots, flyers, and images with text plastered across them get filtered by Google and clutter your profile for no benefit.

Photos of a location you no longer occupy are quietly costing you customers. If you renovated two years ago, the old waiting room needs to come down.

Technical details that matter

Upload photos at a reasonable size, roughly 1200 pixels wide or larger, in JPG or PNG format. Shoot horizontal for cover and exterior images, since that is how Google displays them. Take them on a real camera or a recent phone in good light, and take them yourself rather than pulling them off your website, where they have often been compressed down.

Do not bother renaming files with keywords. Google reads the image, not the filename, and that particular piece of advice has been outdated for years.

The habit that separates active listings from dead ones

Google favors businesses that show signs of life. Uploading twenty five photos once and never returning gives you a good first impression and nothing after that.

Build a simple rhythm instead. Add three to five new photos every month. A finished project. A new piece of equipment. A staff member who just joined. The renovated reception area. It takes fifteen minutes and it keeps your profile looking current to both Google and the person deciding whether to call you.

One more habit worth building: check your customer uploaded photos monthly. Anyone can add a photo to your listing, and a dark, crooked shot from a customer can end up representing your business. You cannot delete those directly, but you can report the ones that violate Google's policies, and you can bury weak ones by uploading better images consistently.

The bottom line

Photos will not carry a listing on their own. They will not fix bad categories, thin reviews, or business information that disagrees across the web. What they do is convert the attention you already earned. Someone found you, tapped your listing, and gave you ten seconds. Photos decide what happens next.

Most businesses can fix this in an afternoon with a phone and decent light, then keep it alive with fifteen minutes a month. Few things in local marketing pay back that quickly.
