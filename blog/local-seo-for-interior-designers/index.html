Local SEO for Interior Designers: How to Win Clients Who Search Locally

Most interior design clients start the same way. A homeowner buys a house in Pasadena that needs a full refresh, or finally commits to redoing the kitchen, and types "interior designer near me" into Google. They look at the map, scroll through a few portfolios, read some reviews, and reach out to two or three firms. If your studio is not in that first look, your work never gets the chance to speak for itself.

Interior design is a visual business, so many designers put most of their energy into Instagram and Houzz. Those channels matter. But the client who is ready to hire, with a budget and a timeline, very often starts with a local search. Here is how to make sure your firm is the one they find.

Treat your Google Business Profile like a second portfolio

Your Google Business Profile is the listing that appears on the map and in the panel beside the search results. For a designer, it is often the first place a prospect sees your work. Most designer profiles we review have a logo, a phone number, and a handful of photos from years ago.

The fix: Choose "Interior designer" as your primary category, then add secondary categories only when they truly fit, such as "Kitchen remodeler" if you manage full kitchen projects. Fill in every field, write a description that names your style and the neighborhoods you serve, and upload finished project photos on a regular schedule. A designer who adds a few new rooms every month gives Google fresh activity and gives prospects a reason to keep looking.

Build a page for each service you actually sell

A homepage that says "We design beautiful spaces" tells Google almost nothing. Clients search for specific things: kitchen designer in Brentwood, commercial interior design in Los Angeles, home staging in Sherman Oaks. If your site has no page that matches those searches, you are asking Google to guess, and it usually guesses in favor of a competitor who spelled it out.

The fix: Create a dedicated page for each core service, whether that is full home design, kitchen and bath, hospitality and commercial work, or staging. Each page should explain what the service includes, who it is for, and where you work, with photos from real projects of that type. Name the city or neighborhood in the page title and the opening paragraph.

Show where you work without stuffing in city names

Designers in Los Angeles often serve a wide area, from Malibu to Pasadena. Google tends to favor businesses close to the person searching, so a studio in Studio City can struggle to appear for someone searching from Santa Monica. You cannot move your office for every search, but you can show Google that you do real work in those places.

The fix: Write short project stories tied to a location. A page about a Spanish Colonial Revival refresh in Los Feliz is useful to a prospect who loves that style, and it tells Google you work in Los Feliz. That beats a footer crowded with twenty city names, which does nothing for readers and can look like spam to search engines.

Make reviews part of every project closeout

Design clients spend serious money and live with the results for years, so they read reviews carefully. Google also weighs the number, quality, and freshness of your reviews when deciding who appears on the map.

The challenge is that design projects take months, and a busy residential firm might only finish a couple dozen a year. That makes every review count.

The fix: Build the review request into your final walkthrough. When the client is standing in their finished living room, thrilled with how it turned out, that is the moment to ask. Follow up the same day with a text that links directly to your review page. Invite them to mention the type of project and the neighborhood, because specific reviews help both future clients and your rankings. Then reply to every review personally and reference the project, so readers can see the care you bring to each relationship.

Keep your directory listings consistent

Houzz, Yelp, Apple Maps, Bing, professional association directories, and local design guides all list your business. Google checks those listings against your profile. If your old studio address still shows on Houzz, or one listing has a different phone number, Google trusts your information less and your map ranking suffers.

The fix: Pick one exact version of your business name, address, and phone number, and make sure it matches everywhere. If you have moved studios or changed numbers in the last few years, this is almost certainly worth an afternoon of cleanup.

Help Google read your portfolio

Your photos are your strongest asset, but Google cannot appreciate a beautiful room the way a client does. It reads file names, alt text, captions, and the words around each image. A gallery of photos named with camera defaults and no written context is a missed opportunity.

The fix: Rename image files to describe the room and the location before you upload them. Add short alt text to each photo, and write a sentence or two for every project covering the style, the scope, and the neighborhood. You keep the visual impact that wins clients over, and you give search engines something they can actually understand.

The bottom line

Interior designers do not have to choose between beautiful branding and being found. The firms that win local search usually treat Google as an extension of their portfolio: a complete profile, service pages that match how clients search, project stories tied to real neighborhoods, and a steady stream of thoughtful reviews. None of it requires a huge budget. It requires consistency, and it builds on itself, because every finished project adds another photo, another page, and another happy client vouching for your work.
