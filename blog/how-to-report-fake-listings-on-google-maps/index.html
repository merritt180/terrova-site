How to Report Fake Listings on Google Maps (And Win Back Your Spot)

You search for your own service in your own neighborhood and see a business you have never heard of sitting in the top three. No storefront, no website worth mentioning, and a name stuffed with keywords like "Best Personal Injury Lawyer Los Angeles." If that sounds familiar, you are probably looking at a fake listing, and it is taking calls that should be yours.

Fake and spammy listings are one of the most frustrating parts of local search, especially in competitive, high-value categories. The good news is that Google gives business owners real tools to report them, and when you use those tools the right way, the results can be dramatic. Here is how to spot the problem, report it properly, and protect your position going forward.

Why fake listings show up in the first place

Google Maps rewards businesses that look relevant and close to the person searching. Spammers know this. They create listings at virtual office addresses, mailbox stores, or residential addresses they have no right to use, and they pack the business name with the exact phrases customers type. Some lead generation companies create dozens of these listings and sell the calls to whoever pays.

This happens most in categories where a single customer is worth a lot of money. Personal injury law, garage door repair, locksmiths, roofing, and luxury car rentals are common targets. A law firm that has spent years earning its reputation can get pushed down by a listing that was created last month, simply because the fake one has the right words in its name.

How to tell if a competitor listing is fake

Not every competitor you dislike is breaking the rules, so start by looking for clear signs. A few questions will usually tell you what you need to know.

Does the address make sense? Drop the address into Street View. If it points to a UPS Store, a shared coworking building with no signage, or a house in a quiet residential block, that is a red flag. Businesses that serve customers at their own location are supposed to have a real, staffed place for those customers to visit.

Is the business name stuffed with keywords? Google's guidelines say your profile name should match your real-world name, the one on your signage and your paperwork. A name like "Smith Law Group Car Accident Attorney Downtown LA" is a violation unless that is truly what the sign says.

Is the same phone number or website tied to several listings? Search the phone number in Google. If it shows up on five different businesses with five different names, you have likely found a lead generation network.

Do the reviews look manufactured? A burst of five-star reviews in a short window, all vague and from profiles with no other activity, is worth noting. It is not proof by itself, but it supports a bigger case.

Step one: suggest an edit directly on the listing

For a simple problem, like a keyword-stuffed name or a listing for a business that has closed, start on the listing itself. Open the listing in Google Maps, choose the option to suggest an edit, and update the name to what the real business is called, or mark it as not existing at this location.

This works best for one or two listings with obvious issues. It is quick, and sometimes the change goes through within a few days. Do not expect it to solve a larger spam network, though. For that you need the next step.

Step two: use Google's Business Redressal Complaint Form

Google offers a dedicated form for reporting businesses that violate its guidelines. You can find it by searching for "Google Business Profile redressal form." This is where you send the serious cases, and it is reviewed by people rather than algorithms.

The key to getting results is building a clear, organized case. Include the listing links, a short explanation of the specific guideline each one breaks, and supporting evidence such as Street View screenshots, state business registration lookups that show no such company exists, or proof that one phone number serves multiple listings. Keep your tone factual. The reviewer needs to see the problem quickly, not read a complaint about how unfair it feels.

Be patient. Reviews can take a few weeks, and you may not get a detailed reply. Check the listings periodically to see whether they have been removed or corrected.

Step three: keep your own profile spotless

Before you report anyone, look hard at your own listing. If your profile name includes extra keywords, your address is a mailbox, or your categories stretch the truth, fix those first. Reporting a competitor invites attention to your whole category, and you want to be the business that clearly follows the rules.

A clean profile is also the best long-term defense. Use your real business name, a verified address or a properly set service area, accurate hours, and categories that truly fit. Then keep earning steady reviews from real customers. A med spa or dental practice with a full, honest profile and fresh reviews every month is very hard for a fake listing to outrank for long.

What to expect after a report

When a fake listing comes down, the effect can be immediate. Every legitimate business in that search moves up a spot, and the calls that were going to a phantom lead seller start reaching real providers again. In crowded categories, clearing out two or three fake listings can be the difference between appearing on the map and being invisible.

Spammers do come back, so treat this as ongoing maintenance rather than a one-time cleanup. A quick monthly check of your most important searches will catch new fakes before they settle in.

The bottom line

You should not have to compete with businesses that do not exist. Learn the warning signs, report clear violations with solid evidence, and keep your own profile honest and active. It takes some patience, but protecting your spot on the map is one of the highest value things an established local business can do.
