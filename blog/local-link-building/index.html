Local Link Building: How to Earn Backlinks That Help You Rank Locally

When Google decides which three businesses to show on the map for "med spa Beverly Hills," it looks at more than your profile and your reviews. It also looks at who on the internet vouches for you. Every time another website links to yours, that is a small public endorsement. For a local business, the endorsements that matter most come from close to home.

This is what people mean by local link building. It sounds technical, but it is really the relationships you already have, turned into links on the web. Here is how it works and where to find the links worth having.

What a backlink actually is

A backlink is simply a link from someone else's website to yours. Google has treated links as votes of confidence since its earliest days, and that has not changed. One link from your local chamber of commerce or a respected neighborhood publication is worth more to a Studio City dental practice than a hundred links from random sites on the other side of the world.

Google is really asking two questions about every link. Is this site trustworthy? And is it related to you? For a local business, related often means geographically related. A link from a Pasadena news site tells Google you are a real part of the Pasadena community.

Why established businesses have an advantage

If you have been in business for five, ten, or twenty years, you have built dozens of relationships that could become links. Vendors you buy from. Organizations you sponsor. Professional associations you belong to. Local reporters who once quoted you. Most of those relationships never made it onto the web as links, which means you are sitting on ranking power you have never used.

That is the heart of this approach. You are not chasing strangers. You are asking people who already know and trust you to say so publicly.

Seven places to find local links

1. Professional associations. Law firms often appear in county bar association directories. Dentists are listed by state and regional dental societies. Home builders belong to builder associations that publish member lists. Check whether your listing links to your website, and if it does not, ask for it.

2. Chambers of commerce and business groups. Membership usually comes with a directory profile that links back to you. It is one of the easiest local links to earn, and it tells Google exactly which city you serve.

3. Sponsorships and community involvement. Youth sports leagues, school fundraisers, charity galas, and neighborhood events almost always thank sponsors on their websites. If you are already writing the check, make sure the thank you includes a link. A luxury car rental company that donates a weekend rental to a charity auction deserves credit on the event page, with a link attached.

4. Vendors and brand partners. The companies you buy from often have pages that feature their clients or authorized providers. A med spa that carries a well-known skincare or injectable brand may qualify for that brand's provider locator. A home builder can ask the architect, cabinet maker, or landscape designer they work with to feature a finished project and link back.

5. Local press. Neighborhood publications and local news sites are always looking for expert sources. A family law attorney who can explain a new state law in plain English, or a dentist who can talk about back to school checkups, makes an easy story for an editor. Those articles usually link to your site.

6. Scholarships and awards. A small local scholarship often gets listed on school financial aid pages, and local business awards usually link to winners.

7. Referral partners. Real estate teams work with lenders, inspectors, and stagers. General dentists refer to specialists. Many of these partners keep a trusted partners or resources page.

What to avoid

Anyone selling links in bulk is selling you a problem. Google is very good at spotting paid link schemes, networks of throwaway blogs, and pages stuffed with hundreds of unrelated links. At best those links get ignored. At worst they drag your site down, and cleaning up the damage can take months.

Here is a simple test. Would this link make sense if Google did not exist? A link from your bar association makes sense. A link from an overseas gambling blog does not. If a link would only exist to game the rankings, skip it.

How to get started this month

Open a blank document and list every organization you belong to, every group you sponsor, every vendor you work with regularly, and every partner who sends you referrals. Most established businesses come up with twenty or thirty names in an hour.

Next, visit each of those websites and search for your business name. You will usually find three situations. Some already link to you, which is great. Some mention you without a link, and those are the easiest wins because you only need to ask for the link to be added. Some do not mention you at all but reasonably could, and those take a friendly email or phone call.

When you ask, keep it short and personal. Something like, "We noticed you list us on your sponsors page. Would you mind linking our name to our website so people can find us?" Most people say yes, because it costs them nothing and takes two minutes.

Aim for a few quality links each month rather than a flood all at once. Steady growth looks natural, and natural is what Google rewards.

The bottom line

Your Google Business Profile, your reviews, and your website tell Google who you are. Local links tell Google that your community agrees. For a business that has been around a while, those links are usually already there in the form of relationships, just waiting to be claimed. A little organized effort turns years of goodwill into better visibility on the map.
