Google Business Profile Q&A: The Section Your Competitors Ignore

There is a section on your Google listing that most business owners have never opened. It sits under your photos, right next to your reviews, and it is labeled Questions and answers. Anyone with a Google account can post a question there. Anyone with a Google account can answer it. That includes people who have never set foot in your business.

That last part is the reason this matters. The Q and A section is one of the few places online where a stranger can speak on behalf of your business, in public, on the listing that shows up when someone searches your name.

Why a few short answers carry so much weight

Picture someone comparing three med spas in Sherman Oaks on their phone. They are not reading your website yet. They are skimming the map listing: rating, photos, hours, and whatever text is sitting there. If one of those three has clear answers to the questions everyone asks, that business feels easier to deal with before a single call is made.

Google also reads that text. The words in your questions and answers are part of what Google associates with your business, and they are written in the exact language real customers use. Someone searching "does the dentist in Studio City do sedation" is using phrasing that rarely appears anywhere on a polished website, but it shows up naturally in a Q and A.

The quiet risk of leaving it empty

An empty section is not neutral. It is an open microphone.

We see this play out in predictable ways. A personal injury firm gets the question "do you handle motorcycle accidents," and a well meaning stranger replies "I believe they only do workers comp." That answer is wrong, it is public, and it can sit there for a year collecting upvotes. A home builder gets asked "do you build on my own lot," and nobody answers at all, so the person moves on to the builder who did answer.

Competitors have been known to plant unflattering questions too. That is rarer than people fear, but a listing nobody is watching is the easiest one to tamper with.

You are allowed to ask your own questions

This surprises most owners. Google permits business owners to post questions on their own profile and answer them. It is a normal, approved way to publish helpful information, and it is the fastest way to fill the section with answers you actually want there.

The mechanics are simple. Ask the question from a personal Google account, then switch to the account that manages the business profile and answer it. The answer will be labeled as coming from the owner, which is exactly what you want, because an owner response reads as authoritative.

The questions worth seeding

Do not invent questions. Use the ones your front desk already answers ten times a day. Those are the real ones, and they are the ones being typed into Google.

For a dental practice: do you take my insurance, do you see kids, do you offer sedation, is there parking, can I be seen same day for an emergency.

For a med spa: is the consultation free, who actually performs the treatment, how long does the appointment take, what is the recovery like.

For an exotic car rental company: what is the minimum age, what insurance do I need, do you deliver to the hotel, is there a mileage limit.

For a home builder: do you build on land I already own, what areas do you serve, what does a typical project timeline look like.

For a law firm: is the first consultation free, do you charge hourly or take a percentage, how quickly will someone call me back.

Six to ten of these covers almost every business. Add them over a few weeks rather than all in one afternoon, so the section reads like it grew naturally.

How to write an answer that does its job

Keep it to a short paragraph. One question, one clear answer, plain language. Mention your city and the service in the way a person would say it out loud, not stuffed in awkwardly. "Yes, we handle motorcycle accident cases throughout Los Angeles County" does more for you than a list of keywords ever will.

End with a next step where it fits. A phone number, a note that you can be reached seven days a week, a mention that consultations are free. You have someone's attention at the exact moment they are deciding. Do not waste it on a one word reply.

Check it, because nobody else will

Notifications for new questions are unreliable, so put it on a calendar. Once a week, open your listing and look at the section yourself. Answer anything new, correct anything wrong, and report anything that is spam or off topic.

One more detail that pays off: questions and answers are sorted by upvotes. Upvoting your best answers helps keep them at the top of the list, where people actually see them.

The bottom line

This takes an hour to set up and a few minutes a week to maintain, which is why so few businesses bother. That is exactly what makes it worth doing. In a crowded map listing, being the business that already answered the question is a real advantage, and right now it is sitting there unclaimed.
