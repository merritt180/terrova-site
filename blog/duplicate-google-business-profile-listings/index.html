Duplicate Google Business Profile Listings: How to Find and Fix Them

Picture a med spa with 60 reviews. Now picture those 60 reviews spread across three different Google listings for the same business, with only 11 of them sitting on the listing customers actually find. Every new patient who looks the place up sees a thin, half empty profile while the strong listing stays buried where nobody scrolls.

Duplicate listings are one of the most common problems we find in audits, and one of the most expensive. They are also quietly invisible. Nothing on your dashboard warns you that a second version of your business exists. You usually notice only when your review count stops making sense, or when someone says they called a number that no longer works.

Where duplicates come from

Almost nobody creates a duplicate on purpose. They appear because Google builds listings from many sources at once. A few of the usual culprits:

You moved. A law firm relocates from one building to another, creates a fresh listing at the new address, and the old one stays live with the old suite number and all the reviews attached to it.

Someone on staff set one up. An office manager at a dental practice cannot find the login, so she makes a new profile. Now there are two, and the original is still verified under a former employee's email.

A vendor created one. Web designers, booking platforms, and listing services all add businesses to Google on behalf of clients. If your new site launched with a slightly different business name, that is often enough to spawn a second entry.

Google generated it. Google builds unverified listings from data it scrapes elsewhere. If a directory lists your exotic rental company at an old warehouse address, Google may create a listing for it without anyone asking.

You have practitioners. Doctors, attorneys, and agents are allowed their own individual listings in some categories, which is legitimate. Where it goes wrong is when three agents on a real estate team each create a listing with the brokerage name and the office address, so Google sees four versions of the same office.

Why it costs you real money

Three things happen when a duplicate is live.

First, your reviews split. Reviews are one of the strongest signals in local ranking and the first thing a prospect reads. A home builder with 38 reviews outranks and outsells one with 14, even when they are the same company.

Second, your ranking gets diluted. Google is trying to decide which listing is the real one, and while it hesitates, neither version gets the full strength of your signals. Sometimes both get suppressed.

Third, customers reach dead ends. The duplicate usually carries an old phone number, a stale address, or hours from four years ago. Someone ready to book calls, gets nothing, and moves on to the next result. You never hear about it.

How to find every copy of your business

Set aside twenty minutes. Do this in an incognito window so your own account does not color the results.

Search your business name in Google Maps and look past the first result. Then search your name plus your city. Then search your main phone number on its own, which is the fastest way to surface a listing sitting under a name you would never think to check. Then search your street address. Finally, search your category and neighborhood, something like "estate planning attorney Encino," and scroll the full list of businesses rather than the top three.

Write down every version you find, including the address, phone number, and review count on each. If you manage multiple locations, do this for every one of them.

Cleaning them up

The right fix depends on what you found.

If the duplicate is unverified, open it in Google Maps, click "Suggest an edit," and choose the option to report it as a duplicate. Google will ask which listing is the correct one. This usually resolves in a few days to a few weeks.

If the duplicate is verified and you can access it, go into your Business Profile manager, find the listing, and remove it. If it holds reviews you care about, request a merge instead of a deletion. Merging asks Google to combine the two listings and carry the reviews over to the one you keep.

If it is verified and you cannot get in, you will need to claim it. Google runs an ownership request process that notifies whoever currently holds the listing. If they do not respond in about a week, you can usually take control. Expect this one to take the longest.

If it is a legitimate practitioner listing, leave it, but clean it up. Use the individual's name rather than the firm name, and make sure the website link points to that person's bio page instead of the homepage. That tells Google these are different entities on purpose.

Then keep it from happening again

Write down one exact version of your business name, address, and phone number, and treat it as the only correct version. Keep the profile login somewhere the business owns, not in a former employee's inbox. Tell any vendor touching your site or booking system not to create listings. And search your own name in Maps once a quarter, because duplicates tend to reappear after a move, a rebrand, or a new website.

It is unglamorous work. It is also one of the few fixes where a single afternoon can visibly change what a customer sees when they look you up tomorrow.
